# Growth · Make It Stick

## Book · ms · Make It Stick: The Science of Successful Learning · Peter C. Brown, Henry L. Roediger III and Mark A. McDaniel, 2014

Why this app's loop works: produce first, test yourself, space it, mix it. Read it so you trust the method on the days it feels slow.

### ms-01 · Rereading is an illusion of knowing
- **Group:** What doesn't work
- **Ask:** You reread a system design article three times and it all feels familiar. Could you now draw the design from memory?
- **Lesson:** Rereading and highlighting are the most popular study habits and among the least effective. They create **fluency**: the text feels familiar, and familiarity feels like mastery. That is an **illusion of knowing**. Familiar is not the same as retrievable under pressure, which is what an interview needs. The trap is that methods that feel productive often aren't, and methods that feel hard (testing yourself) work much better.
- **Plan:** That is why the plan has no rereading: you attempt cold first, compare, then redraw from memory a week and three weeks later.
- **Card:** Why is rereading a trap? ‖ It builds fluency (familiarity) that feels like mastery: an illusion of knowing. Familiar is not retrievable; testing yourself is.
- **Try:** Close this tab and write, from memory, the three most important things in the last article you read.
- **Source:** *Make It Stick*, ch. 1, “Learning Is Misunderstood”. Study habits rated: Dunlosky et al. (2013), *Psychological Science in the Public Interest* 14(1).

### ms-02 · Retrieval practice
- **Group:** What works
- **Ask:** Two groups learn the same material. One rereads it, the other is quizzed on it. A week later, who remembers more?
- **Lesson:** The quizzed group, by a wide margin. **Retrieval practice**, recalling facts or concepts from memory, strengthens memory and interrupts forgetting far more than review. The act of pulling something out of memory changes it: it becomes easier to retrieve the next time. Use quizzes, flashcards, explaining out loud, drawing from memory. One test is not enough: it works best when repeated, with feedback (check the answer after you try).
- **Plan:** Your [flashcards](/study/flashcards), the redraws, Teach-it in the learning loop and the Growth cards in each session all work this way.
- **Card:** What is retrieval practice, and why does it beat review? ‖ Recalling something from memory (quiz, flashcard, redraw, explain aloud). Each retrieval makes the next one easier and slows forgetting; repeat it, with feedback.
- **Try:** After today's concept task, close everything and explain the concept out loud for two minutes before checking.
- **Related:** ms-03, ms-04
- **Source:** *Make It Stick*, ch. 2, “To Learn, Retrieve”. Roediger & Karpicke (2006), *Psychological Science* 17(3).

### ms-03 · Space it out
- **Group:** What works
- **Ask:** You have 6 hours to learn a topic. Is it better to do 6 hours in one day or 1 hour on six different days?
- **Lesson:** Six spaced days, by far. **Massed practice** (cramming) gives quick gains that vanish; **spaced practice** gives slower-feeling gains that last. Spacing works because a little forgetting between sessions makes retrieval harder, and that effort consolidates the memory (it gives time for consolidation, including during sleep). The right gap grows as you learn: a day, a few days, a week, a few weeks. It feels less productive than cramming, which is why people don't do it.
- **Plan:** The [redraw queue](/study/redraws) brings each design back a week and three weeks later; flashcards come back after 1, 3, 7, 14 and 30 days. Trust the gaps.
- **Card:** Massed versus spaced practice? ‖ Cramming feels productive but fades; spaced practice feels slower but lasts, because a bit of forgetting makes retrieval effortful, which consolidates memory.
- **Try:** Pick a problem you solved two weeks ago. Solve it again from scratch, timed.
- **Source:** *Make It Stick*, ch. 3, “Mix Up Your Practice”. A review of spacing: Cepeda et al. (2006), *Psychological Bulletin* 132(3).

### ms-04 · Interleave and vary
- **Group:** What works
- **Ask:** Practising 20 sliding-window problems in a row, or mixing them with heaps and graphs: which feels better, and which works better?
- **Lesson:** Blocking (all of one type in a row) feels better and produces better scores during practice. **Interleaving** (mixing types) feels harder and slower, and produces far better results later, because it trains you to *recognise which kind of problem this is* and pick the method: exactly what an interview tests. **Variation** (practising the same skill under varied conditions) builds flexible knowledge. In Rohrer and Taylor's 2007 study, students who practised mixed volume problems did worse during practice, then scored about three times higher on a test a week later.
- **Plan:** Weeks 7 to 9 are mixed timed sets on purpose. When a mixed set goes badly, it is the method working.
- **Card:** Why does interleaving beat blocked practice? ‖ Mixing problem types trains you to recognise which kind of problem it is and choose a method, the real skill in an interview. It feels worse in practice and works better later.
- **Try:** Make your next three DSA problems three different patterns, in random order.
- **Source:** *Make It Stick*, ch. 3, “Mix Up Your Practice”. Rohrer & Taylor (2007), *Instructional Science* 35(6).

### ms-05 · Generate before you're taught
- **Group:** What works
- **Ask:** Why should you try to design a URL shortener before reading how anyone else did it, when you will probably get it wrong?
- **Lesson:** **Generation**, trying to answer a question or solve a problem before being shown the solution, makes the later learning stick, even when the attempt fails. The struggle prepares the mind: you notice what the solution solves. **Elaboration** (explaining new material in your own words and connecting it to what you already know) and **reflection** (asking what happened, what you'd do differently) deepen it. Errors made while trying are not harmful when you get corrective feedback.
- **Plan:** Step 1 of the [learning loop](/study/loop) (45 minutes cold, before any breakdown) and every derive-first maths task. Each Growth lesson asks you first for the same reason.
- **Card:** What are generation and elaboration? ‖ Generation: attempt before you are taught; even a failed attempt makes the answer stick. Elaboration: explain it in your own words and link it to what you know.
- **Try:** Before the next design, write your full attempt, wrong as it may be, before opening any breakdown.
- **Source:** *Make It Stick*, ch. 4, “Embrace Difficulties”.

### ms-06 · Calibrate: know what you don't know
- **Group:** What doesn't work
- **Ask:** Why are the least skilled people often the most confident in their skill?
- **Lesson:** The **Dunning-Kruger effect**: incompetent people overestimate their competence and fail to sense their mistakes, because the skill needed to judge is the skill they lack. Everyone is vulnerable to illusions of knowing. **Calibration** fixes it: replace subjective feelings with objective measures (a timed test, a mock with a stranger, a redraw checked against the answer). Feedback from tests and from peers tells you where you really stand.
- **Plan:** The scorecard, timed problems, mocks with real people from week 8, redraws checked against your cards: all calibration. When a mock goes worse than you felt it did, that is the instrument doing its job.
- **Card:** What is calibration, and why do you need it? ‖ Replacing the feeling of knowing with objective tests and feedback (timed problems, mocks, checked redraws). Everyone is prone to illusions of competence.
- **Try:** Before your next mock, predict your score out of 30. Afterwards, compare.
- **Source:** *Make It Stick*, ch. 5, “Avoid Illusions of Knowing”. Kruger & Dunning (1999), *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology* 77(6).

### ms-07 · Difficulties that help, and mental models
- **Group:** What works
- **Ask:** If learning feels easy, is that good news?
- **Lesson:** Usually not. **Desirable difficulties** (retrieval, spacing, interleaving, generation) slow you down in the moment and make learning stronger and more durable. Effortful learning changes the brain: making new connections, building **mental models** (structures of knowledge you can apply to new problems, like the six forces). People who extract the underlying rules and principles from examples (structure builders) do better than those who memorise examples. Ability is not fixed: believing you can grow through effort (a growth mindset) predicts how much you will try.
- **Plan:** The six forces and decision cards are mental models: rules that transfer to any design. If a week feels hard, that is the difficulty doing its work.
- **Card:** What are desirable difficulties, and what do they build? ‖ Learning conditions that feel harder (retrieval, spacing, interleaving, generation) but make learning durable; they build mental models that transfer to new problems.
- **Try:** Write down the "rule" behind your last three solved problems, in one line each.
- **Source:** *Make It Stick*, chs. 4 and 7, “Embrace Difficulties” and “Increase Your Abilities”.

### Notes

- **Edition:** Peter C. Brown, Henry L. Roediger III and Mark A. McDaniel, *Make It Stick: The Science of Successful Learning* (Belknap Press of Harvard University Press, 2014).
- **This is the research under the app's own method:** retrieval (flashcards, redraws), spacing (the +7 and +21 day redraws) and generation (the cold attempt).
- **Dunning–Kruger** is debated. Later work argues that part of the effect is a statistical artefact of how the original data were plotted. The practical advice (test yourself rather than trust the feeling) stands either way.
- **Left out:** the chapter on learning styles ("Get Beyond Learning Styles"), whose conclusion is that matching teaching to a preferred style has little support.
