# Growth · Influence

## Book · inf · Influence: The Psychology of Persuasion · Robert Cialdini, 1984 (new and expanded edition 2021)

The science under both the 48 laws and How to Win Friends: seven shortcuts the human mind uses to say yes. Learn them to persuade, and to notice when they are being used to get a yes out of you.

### inf-01 · Reciprocity
- **Group:** Obligation and commitment
- **Ask:** Why do charities mail you a free pen or address labels with their appeal letter?
- **Lesson:** We feel obliged to repay what others give us, even when we did not ask for it and do not like the giver. Gifts, favours and concessions create a debt that people pay back with a yes, often a bigger one. A variant is the **rejection-then-retreat** (door-in-the-face): ask for something large, accept a refusal, then retreat to the smaller request you wanted all along. The retreat feels like a concession, and people reciprocate it. In one of Cialdini's studies, more people agreed to chaperone kids to a zoo for an afternoon after first refusing to volunteer two hours a week for two years.
- **Use:** Give first, without keeping score: review a colleague's PR quickly, share a useful doc, introduce two people. Later asks land on goodwill. In negotiation, concede something you don't care about to create obligation.
- **Spot:** An unsolicited favour, a free sample or a "special exception" right before an ask; a big first request followed by a "reasonable" one.
- **Counter:** Accept the gift, but redefine it in your head: a sales tactic deserves no repayment. Judge the second request on its own.
- **Card:** Reciprocity, and its door-in-the-face variant? ‖ We repay what we receive, even unasked. Rejection-then-retreat: a big ask refused, then a smaller one, which feels like a concession to reciprocate.
- **Try:** Do one useful, unasked favour for someone at work this week, with no ask attached.

### inf-02 · Commitment and consistency
- **Group:** Obligation and commitment
- **Ask:** People who agreed to put a tiny "Be a safe driver" sticker in their window later agreed, far more often, to a huge ugly billboard on their lawn. Why?
- **Lesson:** Once we make a choice or take a stand, we feel pressure to behave consistently with it. Small commitments change the self-image ("I am the kind of person who supports safe driving"), and then larger requests follow from it: the **foot-in-the-door**. Commitments are strongest when they are active, public, effortful and freely chosen. Written ones are especially sticky. This is also why identity-based habits work (ah-04).
- **Use:** Write your study goals and say them to someone (public, written). In a meeting, get small agreements first ("we agree checkout must be idempotent?"). For yourself: tick tiny commitments so the identity grows.
- **Spot:** A small, harmless yes requested early ("you'd agree that growth matters to you?") followed by a large ask that "follows" from it; a low-ball offer that changes after you have said yes.
- **Counter:** Ask yourself: "Knowing what I know now, if I could go back, would I make the same choice?" If not, consistency is a trap; step out.
- **Card:** Commitment and consistency: what makes a commitment stick, and what is the counter? ‖ Active, public, effortful, freely chosen (and written). Counter: "knowing what I know now, would I choose this again?"
- **Try:** Write your weekly study target and send it to one person. That is a public, written commitment.

### inf-03 · Social proof
- **Group:** People and proof
- **Ask:** Hotels that told guests "most guests in this room reused their towels" got more towel reuse than ones that asked them to save the environment. Why?
- **Lesson:** When we are unsure what to do, we look at what people like us are doing. Social proof is strongest under **uncertainty** and when the others are **similar** to us. It works for good (laugh tracks, testimonials, "most people pay on time" letters) and badly (bystanders doing nothing because nobody else is). Note the trap in campaigns that say "so many people do this bad thing": they normalise it.
- **Use:** Show proof from peers: "three teams already use this library" persuades more than a spec. In interviews: "the design we used is how Stripe and Razorpay solve it" calms an uncertain interviewer.
- **Spot:** "Everyone is choosing the premium plan", fake reviews, manufactured queues, a recruiter saying "most candidates accept within 24 hours".
- **Counter:** Check whether the proof is real, and whether those people are really like you. Ask for the numbers.
- **Card:** Social proof: when is it strongest? ‖ Under uncertainty, and when the people we watch are similar to us. Counter: check whether the proof is real and the people really comparable.
- **Try:** Find the community where people like you are preparing for SDE-2 interviews and spend 20 minutes in it this week.

### inf-04 · Liking
- **Group:** People and proof
- **Ask:** Joe Girard sold more cars than anyone in history, and every month he sent every customer a card. What did it say?
- **Lesson:** "I like you." We say yes to people we like, and we like people who are **similar** to us, who **compliment** us, who **cooperate** with us toward shared goals, and who are associated with things we already like. Physical attractiveness and familiarity help too. Tupperware parties work because the buyer likes the friend who hosts. How to Win Friends is, in effect, a manual for this principle.
- **Use:** Find real similarities early in an interview (a shared city, college, problem); be on the same side ("let's figure this out together"). Compliment sincerely.
- **Spot:** You like the salesperson or recruiter a lot, very fast, more than the deal deserves.
- **Counter:** Separate the person from the offer: "Would I take this deal from someone I didn't like?"
- **Card:** Liking: what makes us like someone? ‖ Similarity, compliments, cooperation toward a shared goal, familiarity and good associations. Counter: separate the person you like from the deal.
- **Try:** In your next conversation with someone new, find one real thing you have in common in the first two minutes.

### inf-05 · Authority
- **Group:** People and proof
- **Ask:** In Stanley Milgram's experiments, about two-thirds of ordinary people gave what they believed were dangerous electric shocks to a screaming stranger. What made them do it?
- **Lesson:** A man in a grey lab coat telling them to continue. We are trained from childhood to obey legitimate authority, and we respond to its symbols even when they are empty: **titles, clothes and trappings** (uniforms, cars). Authority persuades best when it is credible on two counts: **expertise and trustworthiness**. A trick to show trustworthiness: mention a small weakness first, then your strength.
- **Use:** Establish your expertise early and naturally: "When I built the Adyen integration at…" Cite real sources. Mention a weakness before a strength ("our design is more complex, but it can't double-charge").
- **Spot:** "Experts recommend…", a title or a brand standing in for evidence, confident delivery with no data.
- **Counter:** Ask two questions: is this person really an expert *on this*? And how truthful are they likely to be here (what do they gain)?
- **Card:** Authority: the two questions that disarm it? ‖ Is this person truly an expert on this topic? How truthful can I expect them to be here (what do they gain)? Titles, clothes and trappings are symbols, not proof.
- **Try:** Write the 15-second sentence that establishes your expertise at the start of an interview.

### inf-06 · Scarcity
- **Group:** Scarcity and unity
- **Ask:** Why does "only 2 left at this price" make you want something you were only lukewarm about?
- **Lesson:** Opportunities seem more valuable when they are less available. We hate to lose freedoms we had, and things that are becoming scarce, or that others compete for, feel best. **Loss** framing beats gain framing: people are more moved by what they stand to lose. Scarcity is the deadline tactic, the "exclusive information", the "one-time offer", and the reason the cookie from the nearly empty jar tastes better.
- **Use:** In negotiation: "I have another process at the final stage, and I'd prefer you." In a proposal: "if we don't fix this before the sale, we lose the 3x traffic window."
- **Spot:** Deadlines that appear only when you hesitate ("exploding offers"); a rush of excitement when something is suddenly limited.
- **Counter:** Notice the rush of arousal as a signal to slow down. Ask what you want the thing for: to use it (scarcity doesn't make it better) or to have it.
- **Card:** Scarcity: why does it work, and what is the counter? ‖ Rare or disappearing things feel more valuable, and losses loom larger than gains. Counter: treat the sudden urge as a signal to slow down and ask what you want it for.
- **Try:** Next time an offer has a deadline, ask for more time. See whether the deadline is real.

### inf-07 · Unity
- **Group:** Scarcity and unity
- **Ask:** Why is a referral from someone at "your" college more powerful than a cold application, even with a weaker resume?
- **Lesson:** We say yes to people we see as one of **us**: the same family, place, tribe or shared identity. Unity is not similarity ("they are like us") but shared identity ("they are of us"). It is created by **belonging** (family, home town, alma mater) and by **acting together** (singing, building, suffering through something in sync). Asking for someone's *advice* rather than their opinion also creates unity, because it puts you on the same side.
- **Use:** For referrals, start with your shared identity: "fellow NIT Durgapur alum". In a design review, ask for advice ("what would you advise?"), not an opinion. Build team rituals.
- **Spot:** "We're family here", "as one of us…", when what is asked would not be asked of family.
- **Counter:** Ask whether the "us" is real and whether family would actually be treated this way.
- **Card:** Unity: how is it different from liking, and how do you create it? ‖ It is shared identity ("of us"), not similarity ("like us"). It comes from belonging (family, place, alma mater) and acting together. Asking for advice creates it too.
- **Try:** Send one alumni message asking for advice, not a favour.
