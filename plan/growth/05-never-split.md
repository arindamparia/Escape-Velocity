# Growth · Never Split the Difference

## Book · ns · Never Split the Difference · Chris Voss, 2016

An FBI hostage negotiator's toolkit. Week 13 asks you to "know your number"; this is how you say it, defend it and get it. The same tools work in design reviews and with difficult stakeholders.

### ns-01 · Be a mirror
- **Group:** Listening
- **Ask:** A recruiter says "the budget for this role is fixed". What happens if you just repeat "fixed?" with a curious tone, and wait?
- **Lesson:** They explain, usually with more information than they meant to give. A **mirror** is repeating the last one to three words (or the critical words) the other person said, as a question. It feels like listening, and people elaborate. Pair it with **tactical empathy**: understand the other side's feelings and mindset, and say it. Use the **late-night FM DJ voice** (slow, low, calm, downward inflection) for statements you want to land; a positive, playful voice for most of the talk.
- **Use:** Mirror whenever you are surprised or stuck: "The budget is fixed?" "…the team needs someone who can start in two weeks?" Silence after the mirror does the work.
- **Script:** Recruiter: "We usually don't go above the band." You (curious): "Above the band?" Then wait four seconds.
- **Card:** What is a mirror in negotiation? ‖ Repeat the last one to three critical words as a curious question, then stay silent: people elaborate and reveal more.
- **Try:** Use three mirrors in conversations today. Write down what each one surfaced.
- **Source:** *Never Split the Difference*, ch. 2, “Be a Mirror”.

### ns-02 · Label their emotions
- **Group:** Listening
- **Ask:** Your manager seems tense about your proposal. What do you say before defending it?
- **Lesson:** A **label** names the other person's feeling or position, starting with "It seems like…", "It sounds like…", "It looks like…" (never "I hear that…", which makes it about you). Labelling a negative emotion defuses it; labelling a positive one reinforces it. After a label, go silent and let it work. It shows you understand without agreeing.
- **Use:** "It seems like you're worried this delays the launch." "It sounds like you've been burnt by rewrites before." Then listen.
- **Script:** Offer call, when the recruiter hesitates: "It seems like there's some pressure to close this role quickly." (Silence.)
- **Card:** How do you label, and what does it do? ‖ "It seems like / it sounds like / it looks like…" a feeling or position, then silence. It defuses negatives and reinforces positives without agreeing.
- **Try:** Label one person's emotion this week in a tense moment, then stay quiet.
- **Source:** *Never Split the Difference*, ch. 3, “Don’t Feel Their Pain, Label It”.

### ns-03 · The accusation audit
- **Group:** Listening
- **Ask:** You are about to ask for 30% more than the offer. What if you said the worst thing they might think about you, before they think it?
- **Lesson:** An **accusation audit** lists every negative thing the other side could think about you, and says them first: "You're probably going to think this is greedy…" Naming the fear takes its sting away, and often the other person rushes to reassure you ("no, no, it's not greedy"). It works because unspoken negatives grow; spoken ones shrink.
- **Use:** Before any ask that might annoy: a salary jump, a deadline push, a big rewrite. Open with two or three accusations, then make the ask.
- **Script:** "You might feel I'm asking for too much, and that I'm not grateful for the offer. I am grateful, and I want to join. Based on the scope we discussed, I'm looking for a base of 42."
- **Card:** What is an accusation audit? ‖ Say the negative things the other side might think about you before they do ("you'll probably think this is greedy"); naming them defuses them.
- **Try:** Write the accusation audit for your future salary ask: the three worst things they might think.
- **Source:** *Never Split the Difference*, ch. 3, “Don’t Feel Their Pain, Label It”.

### ns-04 · Seek "no", beware "yes"
- **Group:** Steering
- **Ask:** Which question is more likely to get an honest answer from a busy senior engineer: "Do you have a few minutes?" or "Is now a bad time to talk?"
- **Lesson:** "Yes" is often fake: a counterfeit yes to get rid of you, a confirmation yes with no commitment. "No" feels safe and gives the speaker control, so people say it more honestly and relax after it. So design **no-oriented questions**: "Is now a bad time?", "Would it be ridiculous to…?", "Have you given up on this project?" (to someone who has gone silent). There are three kinds of yes (counterfeit, confirmation, commitment); you want the last one.
- **Use:** To a recruiter who has gone quiet: "Have you given up on filling this role?" To a reviewer: "Is it a bad idea to merge this today?"
- **Script:** Reviving a stalled referral: "Hi Neha, have you given up on me joining your team?" (It almost always gets a reply.)
- **Card:** Why seek "no"? ‖ "No" feels safe and gives control, so people answer honestly and relax; "yes" is often counterfeit. Use no-oriented questions ("is now a bad time?").
- **Try:** Send one "have you given up on…?" message to a conversation that went quiet.
- **Source:** *Never Split the Difference*, ch. 4, “Beware “Yes”—Master “No””.

### ns-05 · Trigger "that's right"
- **Group:** Steering
- **Ask:** What is the difference between someone saying "you're right" and "that's right"?
- **Lesson:** "You're right" usually means "please stop talking". "That's right" means they feel truly understood, and it is the breakthrough moment. You trigger it with a **summary**: paraphrase their position and label the feelings behind it so well that they can only say "that's right". After it, people are open to your proposal.
- **Use:** In a stuck design debate, summarise the other engineer's view better than they did. In an interview, summarise the requirements back before designing.
- **Script:** "So if I've got it: you need checkout to survive a region outage, you're nervous because last year's failover took an hour, and you'd rather pay for complexity than risk that again." ("That's right.")
- **Card:** How do you trigger "that's right"? ‖ Summarise their position and label the emotions behind it until they feel fully understood. "You're right" is a brush-off; "that's right" is the breakthrough.
- **Try:** In your next disagreement, summarise the other side until they say "that's right" before you argue.
- **Source:** *Never Split the Difference*, ch. 5, “Trigger the Two Words That Immediately Transform Any Negotiation”.

### ns-06 · Bend their reality: anchors and loss
- **Group:** Bargaining
- **Ask:** Your expected number is 40 lakh. Should you say "40", "somewhere between 38 and 45", or something else?
- **Lesson:** Never split the difference: meeting halfway (wearing one black and one brown shoe) is often a bad deal for both. Instead shape what the other side thinks is reasonable. **Anchor** their emotions with an accusation audit, then let *them* name a number first if you can. If you must go first, use a **range** whose bottom is your real target (they will aim at the bottom), use **odd, precise numbers** (41.7 sounds calculated), and frame things as **loss** ("what you'd lose if this hire takes three more months"). Deadlines are often arbitrary; treat them as such.
- **Use:** Ask "what's the range for this role?" first. If pushed: "Based on the market for SDE-2 in fintech, I'm looking at 42 to 46." Then mention non-monetary terms (joining bonus, title, start date).
- **Script:** "What's the budget you have in mind for this role?" … "That's lower than I expected. Based on similar roles at Razorpay and PhonePe, I'm looking at 42 to 46, and I'm flexible on the joining date."
- **Card:** Why never split the difference, and how do you anchor? ‖ Meeting halfway is often bad for both. Let them name a number first; if not, give a range whose bottom is your target, use precise odd numbers, and frame what they lose.
- **Try:** Research the pay band for your target role and write your range, with the bottom as your real target.
- **Plan:** Week 13: [research pay bands and know your number](/weeks/13#w13-06).
- **Related:** fk-08
- **Source:** *Never Split the Difference*, ch. 6, “Bend Their Reality”.

### ns-07 · Calibrated questions
- **Group:** Steering
- **Ask:** The offer is too low. Instead of "can you do better?" (a yes-or-no question), what could you ask?
- **Lesson:** **Calibrated questions** start with "how" or "what" (rarely "why", which sounds accusatory). They hand the problem to the other side and give them the illusion of control, while you steer. The most powerful one: **"How am I supposed to do that?"**, a polite no that makes them solve your problem. Others: "What about this is important to you?", "How can we solve this?", "What's the biggest challenge you face?"
- **Use:** In negotiation and with stakeholders who make impossible demands: "How am I supposed to ship it by Friday without the API from your team?"
- **Script:** Offer too low: "I really want to make this work. How am I supposed to accept a base that's below my current one?"
- **Card:** What are calibrated questions, and the most powerful one? ‖ "How" and "what" questions that make the other side solve your problem while feeling in control. "How am I supposed to do that?" is a polite no.
- **Try:** Turn one "can you…?" request you need to make into a "how" or "what" question.
- **Source:** *Never Split the Difference*, ch. 7, “Create the Illusion of Control”.

### ns-08 · Guarantee execution
- **Group:** Steering
- **Ask:** A recruiter says "yes, I'll check with the hiring manager about the base". How do you know it will really happen?
- **Lesson:** A yes means nothing without implementation. Ask **"how" questions about execution**: "How will we know we're on track?", "What happens if…?". Use the **Rule of Three**: get them to agree to the same thing three times, in different ways (a commitment, a summary, a calibrated question), which makes a fake yes much harder. Watch for the **7-38-55** split (words, tone, body): if the words say yes and the tone says no, it is a no. And find the people **behind the table** who can kill the deal.
- **Use:** "What does the approval process look like from here?" "Who else needs to sign off?" "What could stop this from going through?"
- **Script:** "Great, thank you. Just so I understand: who else is involved in approving the revised offer, and what usually holds it up?"
- **Card:** How do you make sure a yes is real? ‖ Ask how it will be implemented and who else decides; get the same agreement three times (Rule of Three); trust tone and body over words.
- **Try:** In your next agreement at work, ask "what could stop this from happening?"
- **Source:** *Never Split the Difference*, ch. 8, “Guarantee Execution”.

### ns-09 · Bargain hard: the Ackerman model
- **Group:** Bargaining
- **Ask:** Your target is 40. If you have to make offers in steps, in what pattern should the steps go?
- **Lesson:** Voss's **Ackerman model** for the side that pays (reverse it when you are the one asking): set your target; first offer at 65% of it; then 85%, 95% and 100%, with **decreasing increments** (they signal you are reaching your limit). Use empathy and "how" questions between steps to make them counter first. Make the final number **precise and odd** (₹40,37,000 looks calculated), and with the final number add a **non-monetary** item you don't care about, to show you're at your limit.
- **Use:** As the one asking for pay: ask high first, then come down in shrinking steps, never meeting halfway; the final step is small and precise, with a non-cash ask (a review at six months, a joining bonus).
- **Script:** Final round: "I can come to 41.75 if we can agree a compensation review at six months. That's as far as I can go."
- **Card:** The Ackerman model? ‖ Target; offer 65%, 85%, 95%, 100% with shrinking steps; make them counter between; end on a precise, odd number plus a non-monetary item.
- **Try:** Write your own concession plan for the offer call: first ask, three steps, and the non-cash item at the end.
- **Plan:** Week 13: [know your number](/weeks/13#w13-06).
- **Related:** fk-08
- **Source:** *Never Split the Difference*, ch. 9, “Bargain Hard”.

### ns-10 · Find the black swan
- **Group:** Bargaining
- **Ask:** You think the offer is low because "the company is cheap". What unknown might change everything?
- **Lesson:** In every negotiation there are **black swans**: unknown unknowns that, once uncovered, change everything. Maybe the team lost two engineers and is desperate; maybe the hiring manager's bonus depends on filling roles this quarter; maybe the band is fixed but the level isn't. Assume the other side is not crazy: if they seem irrational, they are constrained or have information you lack. Ask, listen, and spend time face to face. Black swans give you **leverage**: positive (what they want), negative (what they could lose) and normative (their own rules and values).
- **Use:** "What happened to the last person in this role?" "What's driving the timeline?" Use their own stated values: "You said you pay top of market for strong candidates…"
- **Script:** "If the band is fixed for SDE-2, is there flexibility in the level, or in a joining bonus?"
- **Card:** What is a black swan, and the three kinds of leverage? ‖ An unknown that changes the whole negotiation, found by asking and listening (assume they are not crazy, just constrained). Leverage: positive, negative, normative (their own rules).
- **Try:** In your next interview, ask "what's driving the timeline to fill this role?" and listen for the swan.
- **Source:** *Never Split the Difference*, ch. 10, “Find the Black Swan”.

### Notes

- **Edition:** Chris Voss with Tahl Raz, *Never Split the Difference: Negotiating as if Your Life Depended on It* (Harper Business, 2016).
- **The 7-38-55 rule** comes from Albert Mehrabian's 1960s studies of how people judge *feelings* from messages where words and tone disagree. It does not mean words carry 7% of all meaning. Use it only as "when words and tone disagree, trust the tone".
- **The Ackerman model** is written for the side that pays. For asking for pay it runs the other way: start high, and come down in shrinking steps.
- **Rupees, not dollars:** the scripts use lakh figures as examples only. Your real numbers come from week 13's pay-band research.
