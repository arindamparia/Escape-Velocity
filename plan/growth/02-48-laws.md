# Growth · The 48 Laws of Power

## Book · law · The 48 Laws of Power · Robert Greene, 1998

How power actually works in any group of people, distilled from three thousand years of courts and offices. Each law comes with the move, how it backfires, how to recognise it when it is done to you, and the counter. Offices are courts with Slack. Where a story below is my illustration rather than the book's, it says so.

### law-01 · Never outshine the master
- **Group:** Above you: masters and courts
- **Ask:** You redesign your manager's flagship system in a week and present it to the director yourself. What could possibly go wrong?
- **Lesson:** People above you need to feel superior. Make them look more brilliant than they are and you rise; make them feel insecure and they will remove you, however good you are. Talent displayed carelessly reads as a threat. So show your brilliance as theirs: frame your idea as building on their thinking, ask their advice on what you already know, and let them present the win. This is not humility, it is safety: the most dangerous person to outshine is a master who is insecure.
- **Story:** Nicolas Fouquet, Louis XIV's finance minister, threw the most lavish party France had seen at his new château to impress the young king. Louis felt eclipsed; within weeks Fouquet was arrested and spent the rest of his life in prison. Galileo, by contrast, dedicated his discovery of Jupiter's moons to the Medici, naming them the Medicean stars, and became their court philosopher.
- **Use:** In a design review, open with "building on what you suggested last quarter…". Send your manager the deck first and offer them the slot. Credit them upward in front of their boss; you keep the substance, they keep the face.
- **Backfires:** With a confident, secure leader, too much deference reads as weakness or flattery. And if you hide all your work, nobody higher up will know you did it: make sure the right people learn it was you, quietly.
- **Spot:** A senior who keeps asking for "just the doc" and then presents it alone; a lead who goes cold after you got praised in an all-hands.
- **Counter:** Keep written traces (design doc authorship, PR history). Make your work visible sideways, to peers and skip-levels, without ever making your manager look small.
- **Card:** Law 1, never outshine the master: the move? ‖ Make those above you feel superior: present your brilliance as theirs, ask their advice, let them take the stage. Talent shown carelessly reads as a threat.
- **Try:** In your next update to your manager, frame one of your ideas as building on something they said. Notice the reaction.

### law-02 · Never put too much trust in friends; learn how to use enemies
- **Group:** Allies, friends and enemies
- **Ask:** You need a strong deputy. Your closest friend on the team wants the role; a sharp colleague who once argued against you also does. Whom do you trust more to deliver?
- **Lesson:** Friends are dangerous in business: they envy you more easily, they take favours for granted, and you cannot fire them without losing the friendship. A former enemy you win over has more to prove and will be more loyal, because they must keep showing that your trust was right. And open enemies are useful: they keep you sharp and define what you stand for. Keep friends for friendship; hire for competence.
- **Story:** The Byzantine emperor Michael III raised his friend Basil from stable groom to chamberlain and then co-emperor. Basil had him murdered and took the throne. Chinese emperors often bought the loyalty of rebellious generals by giving them commands; the rehabilitated enemy had everything to prove.
- **Use:** When you build a team or a working group, pick the sceptic who challenged your design: give them a real role and they become your best defender. Keep business promises in writing, even with friends.
- **Backfires:** Reconciled enemies can also be waiting for revenge; watch actions, not words. And treating friends as suspects makes you lonely: the law is about not *mixing* friendship and business, not about trusting nobody.
- **Spot:** A friend who expects the plum task because you are friends; a colleague who stays close to you while quietly competing for the same promotion.
- **Counter:** Separate the channels: friendship over chai, business with clear expectations, and judge both by what is delivered.
- **Card:** Law 2: why distrust friends and use enemies? ‖ Friends envy more and expect favours; a won-over enemy has everything to prove and is more loyal. Keep friendship and business separate.
- **Try:** Think of one person who once disagreed with you at work. Ask them for their honest view on something you are building.

### law-03 · Conceal your intentions
- **Group:** Concealment and words
- **Ask:** You plan to leave your job in three months. Who should know, and when?
- **Lesson:** Keep people off balance and in the dark by never revealing the purpose behind your actions. If they do not know what you are after, they cannot prepare a defence. Use decoys: an apparent goal, a show of sincerity, a smoke screen of friendly openness. People prefer the familiar, so a visible purpose that seems ordinary hides an unusual real one. The more you reveal, the more you can be blocked.
- **Story:** In 1850, Otto von Bismarck, known as a fierce Prussian militarist, gave a speech in favour of peace with Austria that astonished everyone. It won him the king's trust and the post of Prussian envoy, and his real aim, Prussian dominance over Austria, which he pursued later through war.
- **Use:** Interviewing elsewhere: say nothing until the offer is signed. Going for a promotion: talk about the impact you want to have, not the title. In a negotiation, your walk-away point and your deadline are never shared.
- **Backfires:** Once you are known for hidden motives, every word you say is distrusted. Over-concealment with your own team kills collaboration; reserve it for competition, not cooperation.
- **Spot:** A colleague very interested in your plans who shares nothing of their own; a sudden change in someone's friendliness right before reviews.
- **Counter:** Watch what people do, not what they say, and ask yourself whom an action benefits. Give little away until you know the purpose of the question.
- **Card:** Law 3, conceal your intentions: why? ‖ If people do not know what you want, they cannot defend against it. Use an ordinary visible purpose to hide the real one; never reveal your walk-away point or your next move.
- **Try:** In your next negotiation of any size (a deadline, a price), decide in advance what you will not reveal, and hold it.

### law-04 · Always say less than necessary
- **Group:** Concealment and words
- **Ask:** In a meeting with a director, you have four things to say and two minutes. Do you say all four?
- **Lesson:** The more you say, the more common you appear and the less in control. Powerful people impress by saying less; silence makes others nervous and they fill it, often giving away more than they meant. The more you say, the more likely you are to say something foolish. Short, vague-on-purpose answers keep you in command; once words are out, you cannot take them back.
- **Story:** The Roman war hero Coriolanus was so admired that he was a certain winner for consul, until he made speech after speech insulting the common people. They exiled him. Louis XIV, by contrast, answered most requests with a single "I shall see", and no one could read him.
- **Use:** In a salary negotiation, after you state your number, stop talking. In an interview, answer the question asked, then pause; let them ask the follow-up. In design reviews, one crisp point lands harder than five.
- **Backfires:** In an interview or a standup, too little information reads as evasive or unprepared. Silence works from strength; from weakness it looks like you have nothing. Clarity is not over-talking.
- **Spot:** Someone who answers your long explanation with "hmm" and waits, so you keep revealing more.
- **Counter:** Notice the silence and let it sit. Say your point once, then ask a question back.
- **Card:** Law 4, say less than necessary: why? ‖ Fewer words sound more powerful and give away less; silence makes others fill the gap. Words cannot be taken back.
- **Try:** In tomorrow's standup, give your update in one sentence and stop.

### law-05 · So much depends on reputation: guard it with your life
- **Group:** Reputation and attention
- **Ask:** Two engineers write equally good code. One is known as "the person who never breaks prod". Who gets the critical project?
- **Lesson:** Reputation is the cornerstone of power. With it you can intimidate and win without fighting; once it slips, you are vulnerable everywhere. Build it on one outstanding quality (reliability, speed, depth) and protect it. Be alert to attacks and see them coming. And understand that you can attack a rival's reputation, though in the open it often rebounds.
- **Story:** In the legend of Zhuge Liang's empty fort, the strategist, his army away, faced a huge enemy force. He opened the city gates, sat on the wall playing the lute. The enemy general, knowing his reputation for cunning, assumed a trap and retreated. P. T. Barnum built his museum's fame while ruining a rival's with rumours and stunts.
- **Use:** Choose your quality: "the one who writes the incident postmortem everyone reads", "the payments person". Deliver on it every time. Never let a slip go unaddressed; own it fast and visibly.
- **Backfires:** A reputation built on one quality becomes a cage (always on call because you are reliable). Attacking another's reputation makes you look petty and invites revenge.
- **Spot:** Small comments about your reliability that start appearing in rooms you are not in.
- **Counter:** Answer an attack calmly with evidence, never with matching gossip. Usually the best counter is a visible result.
- **Card:** Law 5: why guard your reputation? ‖ It wins battles before they start and is the one thing that makes you safe or vulnerable. Build it on one standout quality and defend it.
- **Try:** Write the one quality you want people at work to name when they describe you. What did you do this week that proves it?

### law-06 · Court attention at all costs
- **Group:** Reputation and attention
- **Ask:** Your work is excellent, but no one outside your team knows it exists. Is that a problem?
- **Lesson:** Everything is judged by its appearance; what is unseen counts for nothing. Never let yourself get lost in the crowd. Make yourself a magnet for attention by appearing larger, more colourful, more mysterious than the bland masses. Any attention is better than none, though in a modern office, attention for good work is the attention that compounds.
- **Story:** P. T. Barnum turned curiosity into a business: he exhibited Joice Heth as George Washington's 161-year-old nurse, then planted letters calling her a fake to keep the papers talking. Every controversy filled his museum.
- **Use:** Write the internal blog post about the latency fix. Give the brown-bag talk. Post the design doc in the big channel. Put a number in the headline: "p99 down 40%".
- **Backfires:** Attention-seeking without substance is noticed fast. In engineering cultures, self-promotion is distrusted; let the results be loud and your tone quiet. Shock tactics damage reputation (law 5).
- **Spot:** Someone who always has the demo but rarely the code.
- **Counter:** Ask for the details publicly and kindly. Substance survives questions; noise does not.
- **Card:** Law 6, court attention: the move for an engineer? ‖ What is unseen counts for nothing: make your work visible (posts, talks, numbers in headlines) and stand out from the crowd, with substance underneath.
- **Try:** Write a three-line post about something you shipped this quarter, with one number in the first line.

### law-07 · Get others to do the work for you, but always take the credit
- **Group:** Leverage and dependence
- **Ask:** Tesla invented much of what made Westinghouse and Edison rich, yet died poor. What did he not understand?
- **Lesson:** Use the wisdom, knowledge and legwork of others to further your cause. It saves time and energy and gives you a reputation for efficiency and speed. In the end your helpers are forgotten and you are remembered. Never do yourself what others can do for you. In engineering terms: leverage. Seniors are judged by what they make happen, not what they type.
- **Story:** Nikola Tesla worked for Edison, was promised $50,000 for redesigning his dynamos, and was told it was "American humour". Edison's name stayed on the work. The painter Rubens ran a workshop where assistants painted much of each canvas; he added the finishing touches and the signature.
- **Use:** The legitimate version: delegate, then present the team's result as the work you led. Be the person who stitches others' work into a launch. Reuse existing libraries, docs and people's past designs instead of starting from zero.
- **Backfires:** In a modern team, stealing credit is found out fast and destroys trust (and makes you the target of law 7 used against you). Leaders who give credit get more help next time. Take the credit for *leading*, give the credit for *doing*.
- **Spot:** Someone who "aggregates" others' work into their own deck without names; your idea resurfacing in a senior's slide.
- **Counter:** Put your name on your work early and in writing: the design doc's author line, the PR, a summary email "as I proposed in…". Present your own work when you can.
- **Card:** Law 7, others do the work, you take the credit: the legitimate use and the counter? ‖ Leverage others' effort and lead the result; be remembered as the one who made it happen. Counter it by putting your name on your work early, in writing.
- **Try:** Check your last big piece of work: is your name on it somewhere a senior would see? If not, fix that this week.

### law-08 · Make other people come to you: use bait if necessary
- **Group:** Leverage and dependence
- **Ask:** In a negotiation, who has the advantage: the one who travels to the other's office, or the one who is visited?
- **Lesson:** When you force the other person to act, you are the one in control. It is always better to make your opponent come to you, abandoning their own plans. Lure them with fabulous gains, then attack. You hold the cards. Making the other side move first, on your ground and your terms, makes them spend energy while you keep yours.
- **Story:** In Greene's telling, Talleyrand helped lure Napoleon out of exile on Elba by letting him believe France was ready to welcome him back; Napoleon's return ended at Waterloo and permanent exile.
- **Use:** Build a public track record (a blog, an open-source library, a known talk) so recruiters and teams come to you. In a negotiation, have them make the first offer; in a meeting, set the agenda.
- **Backfires:** Bait that is obviously bait insults people. Waiting for others to come can mean waiting forever if you have nothing they want.
- **Spot:** An offer that seems too generous and asks you to move fast, on their schedule.
- **Counter:** Slow down. Ask what is in it for them, and move on your own timeline.
- **Card:** Law 8, make others come to you: why? ‖ The one who makes the other side act controls the game; they spend energy on your ground while you keep yours. Use bait: something they want.
- **Try:** Name one thing you could build or publish that would make the companies you want come to you.

### law-09 · Win through your actions, never through argument
- **Group:** Timing and attack
- **Ask:** You are sure the team's chosen database is wrong. Do you win more by arguing it out in the meeting, or by something else?
- **Lesson:** Any momentary victory you win through argument is a Pyrrhic one: the resentment and ill will you stir up outlast any change of opinion. It is much more powerful to get others to agree with you through your actions, without saying a word. Demonstrate, do not explicate. People believe what they see, and nobody likes to lose an argument in public.
- **Story:** The Florentine official Piero Soderini told Michelangelo that his David's nose was too big. Michelangelo climbed the scaffold with a little marble dust in his hand, pretended to chisel, let the dust fall, and asked how it looked now. "Much better," said Soderini, and the statue stayed exactly as it was.
- **Use:** Instead of arguing about the database, build a 1-day benchmark and show the numbers. Instead of debating an approach in review, ship a small prototype behind a flag.
- **Backfires:** Some decisions need discussion and can't wait for a prototype; walking off to "prove it" can read as stubborn. Showing someone wrong in action can humiliate them just as much as an argument; let them save face (like Michelangelo did).
- **Spot:** A colleague who never argues but quietly ships the version they wanted.
- **Counter:** Ask for criteria up front ("what would change your mind?") so the decision is about data, not who argues better.
- **Card:** Law 9, win through actions: why not argue? ‖ Arguments breed resentment even when you win; demonstrations convince without a fight. Show, do not explain, and let the other side save face.
- **Try:** Next time you disagree technically, propose a small test instead of arguing, and agree on what result would decide it.

### law-10 · Infection: avoid the unhappy and unlucky
- **Group:** Allies, friends and enemies
- **Ask:** You sit next to someone who complains about the company all day. After six months, what has happened to your own mood and reputation?
- **Lesson:** You can die from someone else's misery: emotional states are as infectious as disease. You may feel you are helping the drowning man, but you are only precipitating your own disaster. The unfortunate sometimes draw misfortune on themselves, and they will draw it on you. Associate with the happy and fortunate instead.
- **Story:** Lola Montez, a dancer and adventuress, left a trail of ruin: lovers disgraced, a newspaper editor killed in a duel, and King Ludwig I of Bavaria, who lost his throne in 1848 partly over his infatuation with her.
- **Use:** Spend your lunch hours with people who are building something. In a team re-org, move towards projects with momentum. Limit time with chronic complainers (be kind, be brief).
- **Backfires:** Cutting off everyone who struggles makes you cold, and a teammate going through a bad patch is not "infectious". The law is about chronic patterns, not temporary bad luck.
- **Spot:** A colleague who pulls you into every grievance and makes their enemies yours.
- **Counter:** Stay friendly and neutral; do not take sides in their fights; change the subject to something constructive.
- **Card:** Law 10, infection: what to do? ‖ Moods and misfortune spread. Spend your time with people who are energetic and lucky; keep chronic complainers at a kind distance.
- **Try:** List the five people you spend most work time with. Who leaves you with more energy, and who with less?

### law-11 · Learn to keep people dependent on you
- **Group:** Leverage and dependence
- **Ask:** Two engineers: one documents everything so anyone could replace them; one holds knowledge nobody else has. Who is safer in a layoff?
- **Lesson:** To keep your independence you must always be needed and wanted. The more you are relied on, the more freedom you have. Make people depend on you for their happiness and prosperity and you have nothing to fear. Never teach them enough that they can do without you. Be indispensable in something that matters.
- **Story:** An illustration: in the 1990s, many banks kept COBOL programmers on high fees for decades because no one else understood the core systems. Their power came from being the only ones who could keep them running.
- **Use:** The healthy version: own a critical, hard-to-replace domain (payments reconciliation, the deploy system) and be the best at it. Be the bridge between two teams that do not talk.
- **Backfires:** Hoarding knowledge makes you a bottleneck that managers want to remove, and blocks your promotion (you cannot be moved up if no one can replace you). Today, the dependence to build is on your judgement, not on secret knowledge.
- **Spot:** A colleague who refuses to document, or always has to be in the meeting.
- **Counter:** Insist on documentation and runbooks as part of done; pair on critical systems so no one person is a single point of failure.
- **Card:** Law 11, keep people dependent: the healthy version for an engineer? ‖ Be indispensable in something that matters: own a hard, critical domain and be the person whose judgement is needed. Do not hoard; it blocks your promotion.
- **Try:** What would your team struggle with if you left tomorrow? Is that the thing you want to be known for?

### law-12 · Use selective honesty and generosity to disarm your victim
- **Group:** Leverage and dependence
- **Ask:** Al Capone, a man who trusted nobody, once handed $5,000 to a con man. Why?
- **Lesson:** One sincere and honest move will cover over dozens of dishonest ones. Open-hearted gestures of honesty and generosity bring down the guard of even the most suspicious people. Once your selective honesty opens a hole in their armour, you can deceive and manipulate them at will. A timely gift, a Trojan horse, serves the same purpose.
- **Story:** Count Victor Lustig asked Capone for $50,000, promising to double it in two months. He kept the money in a safe, returned it untouched, and apologised that the deal had failed. Capone, amazed by an honest con man, gave him $5,000, which was what Lustig had wanted all along.
- **Use:** The ethical core is that trust is built by early, visible honesty. Admit a small mistake in your first week; give an honest "I don't know" in an interview, then reason it out. Interviewers trust the rest of your answers more.
- **Backfires:** If the honesty is discovered to be a tactic, you lose everything at once. And generosity that obviously expects return is read as bribery.
- **Spot:** An unexpected gift or confession right before a big ask; a vendor who "admits" a small flaw to sell you the big product.
- **Counter:** Separate the gesture from the decision: thank them, then judge the ask on its own merits, after a pause.
- **Card:** Law 12, selective honesty: how does it work? ‖ One sincere act of honesty or generosity lowers even a suspicious person's guard; then the bigger move is believed. Counter: judge the next ask separately from the gesture.
- **Try:** In your next mock, when you do not know something, say so plainly and reason out loud. Notice whether it costs you anything.

### law-13 · When asking for help, appeal to people's self-interest, never to their mercy or gratitude
- **Group:** Leverage and dependence
- **Ask:** You need a senior engineer from another team to review your design this week. Do you remind them of the favour you did them, or something else?
- **Lesson:** If you need to turn to an ally for help, do not remind them of your past assistance and good deeds: they will find a way to ignore you. Instead, uncover something in your request, or in your alliance with them, that will benefit them, and emphasise it out of all proportion. They will respond enthusiastically when they see something in it for themselves.
- **Story:** In 433 BC, Corcyra asked Athens for help against Corinth. Corinth appealed to gratitude and old ties; Corcyra pointed out its large navy and what Athens would gain from it. Athens allied with Corcyra.
- **Use:** "This design touches your service's latency budget; a review now saves you an incident later." "Your team's tool would get its first big user." For a referral: "I'd be a strong hire for your org because… and many companies pay a referral bonus."
- **Backfires:** Some people help out of kindness and are offended by a purely transactional pitch. Know who you are dealing with (law 19): offer both the gain and genuine thanks.
- **Spot:** Someone who keeps reminding you of what they did for you before asking.
- **Counter:** Weigh the request by its own merit; past favours were favours, not debts.
- **Card:** Law 13: how to ask for help? ‖ Appeal to the other person's self-interest, never to mercy or past favours. Find what they gain and make it big.
- **Try:** Rewrite a help request you need to send (a review, a referral) so the first sentence is what they gain.

### law-14 · Pose as a friend, work as a spy
- **Group:** Concealment and words
- **Ask:** What can you learn about a team's real priorities at a dinner that you never hear in their roadmap meeting?
- **Lesson:** Knowing your rival is critical. Use spies to gather information that will keep you a step ahead. Better still, play the spy yourself: in polite social settings, learn to probe. Ask indirect questions to get people to reveal their weaknesses and intentions. Every occasion is an opportunity for artful spying. People talk when they feel liked and relaxed.
- **Story:** An illustration: Talleyrand was famous for his dinners, where he said little and listened much, and diplomats left having told him more than they meant to.
- **Use:** Before an interview loop, talk to people who work there (coffee chats) and ask what the team struggles with. In a new job, have one-to-ones with everyone and listen for what keeps them up at night. Information is leverage.
- **Backfires:** If people feel used, they close up for good and warn others. Do not lie about why you are asking; just listen more than you talk.
- **Spot:** A friendly colleague from another team who asks a lot of specific questions about your project's problems.
- **Counter:** Be warm but keep sensitive details to need-to-know. Ask why they want to know.
- **Card:** Law 14, pose as a friend, work as a spy: what is the move? ‖ Gather information through friendly, relaxed settings and indirect questions; people reveal intentions when they feel liked. Counter: warm but need-to-know.
- **Try:** Have one coffee chat with someone at a company you want to join. Ask what their team's biggest problem is right now.

### law-15 · Crush your enemy totally
- **Group:** Allies, friends and enemies
- **Ask:** Xiang Yu had his rival Liu Bang in his power and let him go. What happened next?
- **Lesson:** All great leaders since Moses have known that a feared enemy must be crushed completely. If one ember is left alight, no matter how dimly it smoulders, a fire will eventually break out. More is lost through stopping halfway than through total annihilation: the enemy will recover and seek revenge. Crush them, in body and spirit.
- **Story:** Xiang Yu, the dominant warlord after the fall of the Qin, repeatedly spared Liu Bang. Liu Bang regrouped, defeated him, and founded the Han dynasty; Xiang Yu died by his own hand.
- **Use:** The modern translation is about problems, not people: when you fix a production incident, fix the root cause completely, not the symptom. When you compete for a role, prepare so thoroughly that there is no second round needed. When you finish a rival design, address every objection in writing.
- **Backfires:** Crushing colleagues in a company is self-destruction: they have friends, the company sees it, and you will need them later. Total victory leaves you with no allies (law 47: know when to stop).
- **Spot:** Someone who will not let a disagreement end, and keeps relitigating your mistakes.
- **Counter:** Don't give them fuel. Close the issue in writing with the decision-makers and move on.
- **Card:** Law 15, crush your enemy totally: the safe translation for work? ‖ Half-measures leave embers that reignite: finish problems completely (root causes, every objection answered). Do not crush colleagues; it costs you allies.
- **Try:** Pick one recurring problem (a flaky test, a repeated bug) and kill its root cause this week.

### law-16 · Use absence to increase respect and honour
- **Group:** Timing and attack
- **Ask:** Why does a colleague who is always available get taken for granted, while one who is hard to get gets listened to?
- **Lesson:** Too much circulation makes the price go down: the more you are seen and heard, the more common you appear. If you are already established, temporary withdrawal makes you more talked about, more admired. Learn when to leave. Create value through scarcity. But the law of absence and presence: first be present enough to be noticed, then absence works.
- **Story:** Deioces, a Mede known for fair judgements, became so sought after that he suddenly withdrew and refused to judge. Lawlessness returned, and the Medes begged him to come back, as king.
- **Use:** Do not answer every Slack message instantly; batch it. Block deep-work time (Deep Work). In job hunting, being "not actively looking" makes you more attractive.
- **Backfires:** Absence before you are established is just being forgotten. At work, unresponsiveness on things you own reads as unreliable, which ruins law 5.
- **Spot:** Someone who makes themselves conveniently unavailable so you come to them on their terms.
- **Counter:** Do not chase. Go round them politely, or set the deadline yourself.
- **Card:** Law 16, use absence: when does it work? ‖ Scarcity raises value: once you are established, being less available makes you more respected. Before you are established, absence just means being forgotten.
- **Try:** Block one two-hour slot this week with notifications off. See whether anything bad happens.

### law-17 · Keep others in suspended terror: cultivate an air of unpredictability
- **Group:** Timing and attack
- **Ask:** Bobby Fischer arrived late, complained about cameras, threatened to leave. Why did it rattle Boris Spassky, the world champion?
- **Lesson:** Humans are creatures of habit with an insatiable need to see familiarity in others' actions. Your predictability gives them a sense of control. Turn the tables: be deliberately unpredictable. Behaviour that seems to have no consistency keeps them off balance, and they will wear themselves out trying to explain your moves. Used to the extreme, this intimidates and terrorises.
- **Story:** In the 1972 World Chess Championship, Fischer's demands and erratic behaviour so unsettled Spassky that the champion's play deteriorated; Fischer won the title.
- **Use:** In a negotiation, vary your pattern: do not always concede in the same increments, and occasionally take a surprising position. In an interview with a tough interviewer, a calm unexpected question back can reset the dynamic.
- **Backfires:** Unpredictable people are not trusted with responsibility, and in a team it reads as unreliable or erratic. Use it with opponents, never with your own manager or team.
- **Spot:** A counterpart whose moods change without reason, so you start working to please them.
- **Counter:** Stay steady and consistent yourself; refuse to guess at their moods; anchor the conversation in facts and dates.
- **Card:** Law 17, unpredictability: where to use it, and where not? ‖ Unpredictable moves keep opponents off balance and wearing themselves out. Use it in negotiations with opponents; never with your own team, where it destroys trust.
- **Try:** In your next negotiation (even a small one), plan your concessions so they shrink unevenly, not in equal steps.

### law-18 · Do not build fortresses to protect yourself: isolation is dangerous
- **Group:** Change and adaptability
- **Ask:** An engineer keeps their head down, eats alone, and only talks in code reviews. Their work is solid. Why are they the first one cut in a re-org?
- **Lesson:** The world is dangerous and enemies are everywhere, so everyone has to protect themselves. A fortress seems the safest. But isolation exposes you to more dangers than it protects you from: it cuts you off from valuable information, makes you conspicuous and an easy target. Better to circulate among people, find allies, mingle. You are shielded from your enemies by the crowd.
- **Story:** Qin Shi Huang, the first emperor of China, grew so afraid of assassination that he hid in his vast palaces and lost touch with his court; when he died, his ministers concealed the death and seized power. Louis XIV, by contrast, kept the whole nobility at Versailles, around him, where he could watch them.
- **Use:** Have one-to-ones outside your team. Join the incident review, the architecture forum. Your network is your early-warning system for re-orgs and openings.
- **Backfires:** Constant circulating with no deep work makes you a socialite with no substance. Balance with law 16 and Deep Work.
- **Spot:** A team or person that has walled itself off ("we don't do meetings") and is losing influence.
- **Counter:** For yourself: one new conversation a week. For a walled-off team: offer a bridge, they need it more than they know.
- **Card:** Law 18: why is isolation dangerous? ‖ The fortress cuts you off from information and makes you an easy target. Stay among people; your network is your protection and your early warning.
- **Try:** Book a 20-minute chat with someone outside your team you have never really talked to.

### law-19 · Know who you're dealing with: do not offend the wrong person
- **Group:** Allies, friends and enemies
- **Ask:** In 1218 the Shah of Khwarezm had Genghis Khan's envoys killed. What did that cost him?
- **Lesson:** There are many kinds of people, and you cannot assume everyone will react the same way. Deceive or outmanoeuvre some people and they will spend the rest of their lives seeking revenge. Greene names types: the arrogant and proud, the hopelessly insecure, the suspicious, the serpent with a long memory, the plain unassuming one who is smarter than they look. Choose your victims and opponents carefully, then never offend or deceive the wrong one.
- **Story:** Shah Muhammad II of Khwarezm insulted Genghis Khan by killing his ambassadors after a trade caravan was massacred. The Mongols destroyed the Khwarezmian empire within a few years; the shah died a fugitive on an island in the Caspian.

### law-20 · Do not commit to anyone

- **Ask:** Elizabeth I never married. Why was that a source of power rather than a weakness?

By maintaining your independence, you become the master of others: playing people against one another, making them pursue you. Courted by many and committed to none, you hold the cards. - **Ask:** Why do con artists so often pretend not to understand what they are selling?
- **Lesson:** No one likes feeling stupider than the next person. Make your victims feel smart, and not just smart but smarter than you are. Once convinced of this, they will never suspect you have ulterior motives. Playing dumber than you are lowers their guard and gets you information and concessions they would never give to someone sharp.
- **Story:** In the Great Diamond Hoax of 1872, two prospectors, Philip Arnold and John Slack, played simple miners who had stumbled on a diamond field. San Francisco bankers and even Tiffany's experts, feeling superior, believed them; the prospectors had salted the field with cheap gems and walked off with hundreds of thousands of dollars.
- **Use:** In a negotiation: "Help me understand how you arrived at this number" (they explain, and often reveal their range). In a new team: ask naive questions; people explain the real politics to someone who seems harmless.
- **Backfires:** In an interview, playing dumb loses the job. Do it rarely and with a purpose; being seen as genuinely slow damages your reputation.
- **Spot:** A counterpart who keeps asking you to "explain it simply", while you give away all your reasoning and constraints.
- **Counter:** Ask your own questions back; share conclusions, not your full reasoning and limits.
- **Card:** Law 21, seem dumber than your mark: what does it buy? ‖ People who feel smarter than you lower their guard and explain more. "Help me understand…" gets information and concessions. Never in an interview.

- **Spot:** A colleague who complains that "nobody here gets it" and is slowly being excluded.
- **Counter:** For yourself: dissent in private, with data, to the right person.
- **Card:** Law 38, think as you like but behave like others: why? ‖ Flaunting contrarian views makes people feel judged, and they punish it. Conform in appearance, share originality with the receptive, and change things from inside.
- **Try:** Find one team convention you dislike. Instead of criticising it, write down how you would improve it from inside, later.

### law-39 · Stir up waters to catch fish
- **Group:** Timing and attack
- **Ask:** In a negotiation, the other side gets visibly irritated. Is that good or bad for them?
- **Lesson:** Anger and emotion are strategically counterproductive. You must always stay calm and objective. But if you can make your enemies angry while staying calm yourself, you gain a decided advantage. Put your enemies off balance: find the chink in their vanity through which you can rattle them, and you hold the strings. Angry people make mistakes and reveal what they want.
- **Story:** An illustration: in many famous chess and negotiation battles, the side that kept its temper while the other lost it won, because the angry side overcommitted and revealed its limits.
- **Use:** Mostly defensive: in a tense review or negotiation, your calm is your advantage. Never let a provocative interviewer or counterpart see you rattled; breathe, slow down, ask a question.
- **Backfires:** Deliberately provoking colleagues is cruel and is remembered (law 19). Provocation in an interview loses the offer.
- **Spot:** Someone deliberately needling you before a decision, so that you react badly in front of others.
- **Counter:** Name it calmly ("I can see this is frustrating; let's look at the data") and refuse to escalate.
- **Card:** Law 39, stir up waters: what is the defence? ‖ Angry people make mistakes and reveal their position, so opponents may provoke you. Stay calm, slow down, and refuse to escalate.
- **Try:** Practise a calm sentence for when you feel provoked in a meeting. Write it and keep it.

### law-40 · Despise the free lunch
- **Group:** Leverage and dependence
- **Ask:** A vendor offers your team a free year of their tool. What is the real price?
- **Lesson:** What is offered for free is dangerous: it usually involves a trick or a hidden obligation. What has worth is worth paying for. By paying your own way you stay clear of gratitude, guilt and deceit. It is also often wise to pay the full price: there is no cutting corners with excellence. Be lavish with your money and keep it circulating, for generosity is a sign and a magnet for power.
- **Story:** An illustration: free tiers of cloud services and tools make teams dependent; the bill arrives once leaving is expensive (lock-in).
- **Use:** Pay for the course, the mock, the coach you actually need; you will use it more seriously. At work, beware "free" help that comes with an expectation of future support for a political ask. Be generous yourself: buy the coffee, share knowledge.
- **Backfires:** Refusing all help out of suspicion leaves you alone (law 18), and paying full price for everything wastes money. Despise the free lunch with strings, not all help.
- **Spot:** A favour you did not ask for, followed by a request you cannot easily refuse.
- **Counter:** Decline gracefully, or pay it back at once so no debt is left open.
- **Card:** Law 40, despise the free lunch: why? ‖ Free things carry hidden obligations or traps; paying keeps you free of debt and guilt. Be generous yourself; generosity attracts power.
- **Try:** Think of a "free" thing (a tool, a favour) you use. What does it really cost you?

### law-41 · Avoid stepping into a great man's shoes
- **Group:** Above you: masters and courts
- **Ask:** You replace a legendary tech lead who just left. Do you run the team exactly as they did?
- **Lesson:** What happens first always appears better and more original than what comes after. If you succeed a great person or have a famous parent, you will have to accomplish double their achievements to outshine them. Do not get lost in their shadow, or stuck in a past not of your own making: establish your own name and identity by changing course. Create a new path.
- **Story:** An illustration: successors who copied a celebrated founder's style were endlessly compared and found wanting; those who changed the direction visibly were judged on their own terms.
- **Use:** Taking over a project or a team: make one visible change of direction early (a new practice, a new goal) so it becomes yours. In interviews, do not lean on "I worked under X"; lead with what you did.
- **Backfires:** Changing things just to be different breaks what worked. Keep what works, change the visible direction.
- **Spot:** A new leader under pressure to "be like the last one".
- **Counter:** Name the predecessor's achievements generously, then state your own direction.
- **Card:** Law 41, avoid a great man's shoes: what to do instead? ‖ The first always looks better than the follower: do not copy a famous predecessor; change direction visibly and build your own name.
- **Try:** If you took over your manager's job tomorrow, what one thing would you visibly do differently?

### law-42 · Strike the shepherd and the sheep will scatter
- **Group:** Allies, friends and enemies
- **Ask:** A whole team resists your migration plan. Do you persuade all ten, or someone in particular?
- **Lesson:** Trouble can often be traced to a single strong individual: the stirrer, the arrogant underling, the poisoner of goodwill. If you allow such people room to operate, others will succumb to their influence. Do not wait for the troubles they cause to multiply, do not try to negotiate with them: they are irredeemable. Neutralise their influence by isolating or banishing them. Strike at the source and the sheep will scatter.
- **Story:** An illustration: in many failed rebellions, removing or winning over the leader dissolved the whole movement overnight; armies without a general rarely keep fighting.
- **Use:** The constructive version: find the opinion leader in a resisting group and win them over first (one-to-one, with their concerns addressed). The others follow.
- **Backfires:** In a company, "banishing" a colleague you dislike is a political fight you can lose badly, and it marks you as dangerous. Win the shepherd over before you try anything else.
- **Spot:** Someone quietly rallying people against your plan in side conversations.
- **Counter:** Bring the disagreement into the open, with them, in a meeting where facts decide.
- **Card:** Law 42, strike the shepherd: the constructive use? ‖ Group resistance usually has one leader. Win or neutralise that person first and the rest follow. In a company, persuade the shepherd before anything harsher.
- **Try:** For your next proposal, name the one person whose support would bring the rest. Talk to them first.

### law-43 · Work on the hearts and minds of others
- **Group:** Change and adaptability
- **Ask:** You can force your team to adopt a new testing practice by mandate. Why might that be the slowest way?
- **Lesson:** Coercion creates a reaction that will eventually work against you. You must seduce others into wanting to move in your direction. A person you have seduced becomes your loyal pawn. The way to seduce others is to operate on their individual psychologies and weaknesses: soften up the resistant by working on their emotions, playing on what they hold dear and what they fear. Ignore the hearts and minds of others and they will grow to hate you.
- **Story:** Marie-Antoinette, a symbol of distance from the hardships of ordinary French people, never won their hearts. When the Revolution came she had no reserve of goodwill at all and was executed in 1793.
- **Use:** Want adoption of your idea? Show each person what it gives them (less on-call, faster deploys), let them try it, tell stories about its wins. Interviews too: make the interviewer *want* to work with you.
- **Backfires:** Emotional manipulation without real benefit is felt and remembered. Seduce with real value.
- **Spot:** A pitch heavy on feelings and light on what actually changes for you.
- **Counter:** Ask: what changes for me concretely, and what does it cost?
- **Card:** Law 43, hearts and minds: why not coerce? ‖ Force creates resistance that comes back later; people moved by their own emotions and interests stay loyal. Win them one by one with what they care about.
- **Try:** Take one thing you want your team to adopt. Write what it gives each of three specific people.

### law-44 · Disarm and infuriate with the mirror effect
- **Group:** Concealment and words
- **Ask:** A difficult interviewer answers your questions with questions. What happens if you calmly do the same back?
- **Lesson:** The mirror reflects reality, but it is also the perfect tool for deception. When you mirror your enemies, doing exactly as they do, they cannot figure out your strategy. The mirror effect mocks and humiliates them, making them overreact. By holding up a mirror to their psyches, you seduce them with the illusion that you share their values; by holding up a mirror to their actions, you teach them a lesson. Greene describes the neutralising, the Narcissus (seductive), the moral and the hallucinatory mirror.
- **Story:** An illustration: negotiators routinely match the other side's tone and pace; a calm counterpart mirroring an aggressive one makes the aggression look absurd.
- **Use:** Build rapport by mirroring language and pace (it is also Never Split the Difference's mirroring: repeat the last three words). Teach a lesson by mirroring a behaviour (if someone always arrives late to your meetings, schedule theirs the same way).
- **Backfires:** Obvious mimicry is creepy or mocking; the moral mirror can start a war.
- **Spot:** Someone who seems to share all your opinions suspiciously quickly.
- **Counter:** Notice the echo; ask them something specific about their own view.
- **Card:** Law 44, the mirror effect: two uses? ‖ Mirror people's values and words to seduce and build rapport; mirror their actions back to neutralise them or teach a lesson. Subtle, never mocking.
- **Try:** In your next conversation, repeat the last few words of what someone said as a question. Watch them explain more.

### law-45 · Preach the need for change, but never reform too much at once
- **Group:** Change and adaptability
- **Ask:** You join a team and see ten things to fix. How many do you change in your first month?
- **Lesson:** Everyone understands the need for change in the abstract, but on the day-to-day level people are creatures of habit. Too much innovation is traumatic and will lead to revolt. If you are new to a position of power, or an outsider trying to build a power base, make a show of respecting the old way of doing things. If change is necessary, make it feel like a gentle improvement on the past.
- **Story:** Thomas Cromwell drove Henry VIII's break with Rome and the dissolution of the monasteries at great speed. The backlash (the Pilgrimage of Grace, enemies at court) built until, after one misstep, Henry had him executed in 1540.
- **Use:** In a new job: change one thing at a time, framed as continuing the team's own direction. In a design, propose an incremental migration, not a rewrite. That is also how real systems survive.
- **Backfires:** Moving too slowly can mean a needed change never happens. Small steps, but steady ones.
- **Spot:** A new leader announcing a revolution in week one.
- **Counter:** Ask what will stay the same; push for phased change.
- **Card:** Law 45, change slowly: why? ‖ People accept change in the abstract and resist it in practice. Respect the old ways in form, and change one thing at a time, framed as improving the past.
- **Try:** Pick one thing you want to change at work. Write the smallest first step that looks like a continuation, not a revolution.

### law-46 · Never appear too perfect
- **Group:** Above you: masters and courts
- **Ask:** A colleague is brilliant, good-looking, promoted fast, and never admits a mistake. How do people around him feel?
- **Lesson:** Appearing better than others is always dangerous, but most dangerous of all is to appear to have no faults or weaknesses. Envy creates silent enemies. It is smart to occasionally display defects, and admit to harmless vices, in order to deflect envy and appear more human and approachable. Only gods and the dead can seem perfect with impunity.
- **Story:** An illustration: Cosimo de' Medici, the richest man in Florence, is said to have dressed and lived modestly and used his money on public works, so that envy never found a target.
- **Use:** Admit a small weakness openly ("I'm terrible at estimating frontend work"); ask for help sometimes. In interviews, a real, specific weakness with what you are doing about it is more credible than a fake one.
- **Backfires:** Admitting real weaknesses in critical areas (I can't do system design) loses the job. Harmless flaws only.
- **Spot:** Quiet resentment around high performers: you, if you never ask for help.
- **Counter:** Share credit, ask for advice, show a harmless flaw.
- **Card:** Law 46, never appear too perfect: why? ‖ Flawlessness breeds silent envy and enemies. Show harmless flaws, ask for help, share credit: it makes you human and safe.
- **Try:** Ask one colleague for advice on something you are genuinely less good at.

### law-47 · Do not go past the mark you aimed for; in victory, learn when to stop
- **Group:** Timing and attack
- **Ask:** You won the design argument. Do you also point out, in the same meeting, the three other places the other engineer was wrong?
- **Lesson:** The moment of victory is often the moment of greatest peril. In the heat of victory, arrogance and overconfidence can push you past the goal you aimed for, and by going too far you make more enemies than you defeat. Do not let success go to your head. There is no substitute for strategy and careful planning: set a goal, and when you reach it, stop.
- **Story:** Cyrus the Great, after conquering much of the known world, attacked the Massagetae beyond the Araxes. Queen Tomyris defeated his army; Cyrus died in the battle (as Herodotus tells it).
- **Use:** In negotiation, when you get the number you aimed for, accept graciously; do not squeeze the last bit and sour the relationship with your future manager. After winning an argument, be generous.
- **Backfires:** Stopping too early leaves value on the table. The skill is setting the right mark in advance.
- **Spot:** You, right after a win, wanting more.
- **Counter:** Write your target before the negotiation; when you reach it, close.
- **Card:** Law 47, stop at the mark: why? ‖ Victory breeds overconfidence; pushing past your goal creates enemies and losses. Set the target in advance and stop when you reach it.
- **Try:** Before your next negotiation, write your target and walk-away. Agree to stop at the target.

### law-48 · Assume formlessness
- **Group:** Change and adaptability
- **Ask:** Your company announces a hiring freeze and a re-org in the same week. Who does best: the rigid planner or the one who adapts?
- **Lesson:** By taking a shape, by having a visible plan, you open yourself to attack. Instead of taking a form for your enemy to grasp, keep yourself adaptable and on the move. Accept the fact that nothing is certain and no law is fixed. The best way to protect yourself is to be as fluid and formless as water: never bet on stability or lasting order. Everything changes.
- **Story:** Sparta built a rigid, closed military state that dominated Greece for a time, but could not adapt to change and declined. Mao Zedong's guerrillas avoided fixed battles and fought where the enemy was not.
- **Use:** Keep your skills portable (systems thinking, not one framework); keep options alive (law 20); in an interview, when the interviewer changes the constraints, adapt cheerfully instead of defending your first design. That is the "break it" step of the learning loop.
- **Backfires:** Total formlessness means no commitments, no identity and no trust. Be fluid in tactics, firm in values and goals.
- **Spot:** You, defending a plan long after the situation changed.
- **Counter:** Revisit plans on a schedule (the Sunday review) and ask what has changed.
- **Card:** Law 48, assume formlessness: what does it mean for a career? ‖ A rigid shape can be attacked; stay adaptable like water: portable skills, open options, cheerful redesign when constraints change. Fluid in tactics, firm in values.
- **Try:** In your next design practice, change one constraint halfway (the break-it step) and redesign without defending the first version.
